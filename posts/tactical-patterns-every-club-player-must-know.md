Tactics are not random flashes of genius. They are recurring geometric facts. The player who spots forks, pins, skewers, discoveries, and overloaded defenders faster will appear sharper even before calculating deeply.

## What This Chapter Teaches
- Recognize the most common tactical motifs that decide club games.
- Use pattern recognition to narrow candidate moves before long calculation.
- Notice the tactical warning signs: loose pieces, king exposure, alignment, and overloaded defenders.
- Link tactics to piece placement instead of seeing them as isolated puzzles.

## Practical Framework
- Scan for forcing moves first, but only after locating your opponent's unprotected pieces and king weaknesses.
- Train with mixed motifs so your brain learns to diagnose the position rather than guess the theme.
- After every tactic, ask what positional weakness made it possible.
- Keep a notebook of tactical misses from your own games. Those are the patterns you are most likely to miss again.

## Model Position 1: A knight fork comes from geometry, not magic

<chess-board fen="r1bqk2r/pppp1ppp/2n5/2b1p3/2B1P1n1/2N2N2/PPPP1PPP/R1BQK2R w KQkq - 6 5" highlight="d5,f7" arrows="e4-d5"></chess-board>
*A jump into the center often attacks more than one target at once.*

Knights become tactical monsters when central squares are available. The point is not to hope for a fork. The point is to recognize when the piece geometry makes a fork plausible.

## Model Position 2: The pin freezes a defender

<chess-board fen="r1bqk2r/pppp1ppp/2n2n2/4p3/1b2P3/2N2N2/PPPP1PPP/R1BQKB1R w KQkq - 4 4" highlight="c3,e1,b4" arrows="b4-c3"></chess-board>
*Pinned pieces distort the whole position because they cannot move freely.*

Once a piece is pinned, the tactical question changes. You no longer ask only what attacks exist now. You ask how many more attackers can be added before the pinned unit collapses.

## Common Mistakes
- Solving puzzles fast without explaining why the tactic works.
- Looking only for your own tactics and missing the opponent's forcing moves.
- Forgetting that loose pieces are tactical targets even in quiet positions.
- Treating calculation and tactical patterns as separate skills.

## Training Work
- Do a short daily puzzle session and sort missed problems by motif.
- In your own games, pause after every tactical sequence and write down the trigger that made it possible.
- Practice on a physical board sometimes so pattern recognition transfers beyond the screen.

## Why This Chapter Matters
Strong tactical play begins before the combination. It begins with spotting the weak squares, loose pieces, and alignments that make combinations available.
