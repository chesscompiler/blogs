Sacrifices are memorable because they compress several strategic truths into one moment. A good sacrifice is not gambling for applause. It is the logical consequence of development lead, king exposure, weak squares, or a decisive endgame transition.

## What This Chapter Teaches
- Separate speculative sacrifices from objectively justified ones.
- Recognize the most common reasons a sacrifice works: king safety, development, piece activity, and structural ruin.
- Calculate the practical horizon after the sacrifice instead of admiring the first move.
- Know when the correct sacrifice is material for time, not material for mate.

## Practical Framework
- Before sacrificing, count attackers and defenders and identify the squares that actually matter.
- Ask what you gain if the sacrifice is accepted, declined, or partially returned.
- Do not sacrifice to avoid making a hard positional decision.
- If the sacrifice leads to a lasting advantage rather than a forced win, describe that advantage concretely before committing.

## Model Position 1: The Greek Gift only works with the right support

*The bishop sacrifice is positional before it becomes tactical.*

This famous sacrifice depends on piece placement, not romance. If the knight cannot jump in, if the queen cannot join, or if defenders remain nearby, the sacrifice often fails.

## Model Position 2: Exchange sacrifices can buy domination

<chess-board fen="r2q1rk1/pp2bppp/2n1pn2/2bp4/3P4/2NBPN2/PPQ2PPP/R1B2RK1 w - - 0 10" highlight="c5,e6,f7" arrows="c3-d5,e6-d5"></chess-board>
*Giving up a rook for a minor piece can be correct if it destroys coordination.*

Not all sacrifices attack the king directly. Sometimes the compensation is a dead bishop, a fixed weakness, or long-term control of dark squares. If you can describe that compensation precisely, the sacrifice becomes much easier to judge.

## Common Mistakes
- Sacrificing because the position feels dull.
- Assuming one attacking move after the sacrifice will be enough.
- Ignoring the defender's simplest resource because the idea looks beautiful.
- Undervaluing endgame compensation when the attack does not lead to mate.

## Training Work
- Collect five sound sacrifices from master games and classify the compensation in each one.
- Review your own speculative sacrifices and ask what evidence you had before playing them.
- Practice calculating acceptance and decline lines, not just the line you want.

## Why This Chapter Matters
A sacrifice is strongest when the position is already asking for it. Learn to read the position first and the sacrifice will often announce itself.
