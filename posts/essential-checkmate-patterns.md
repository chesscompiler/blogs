Many beginners know the rules of chess but still fail to finish won games. The practical cure is not random attacking moves; it is pattern recognition. If you can spot the standard mating nets, you stop chasing ghosts and start converting attacks.

## What This Chapter Teaches
- Tell the difference between check, checkmate, stalemate, and a strong but non-forcing attack.
- Recognize the back-rank mate, ladder mate, smothered mate, and basic queen-king mating net.
- Use forcing moves in the right order: checks first, captures second, threats third.
- See how mating patterns grow out of poor king safety and trapped escape squares.

## Practical Framework
- Start every attacking calculation by counting the king's escape squares.
- Ask which defenders can be removed, pinned, or distracted before you look for brilliance.
- When you are ahead in development, search for forcing lines before settling for a quiet move.
- Practice mating patterns on a bare board so you remember the shape, not just the move order.

## Model Position 1: The back-rank idea

*A rook becomes lethal when the enemy king has no flight square.*

Back-rank mates are not about the rook alone. They are about trapped geometry. If the king's own pawns block escape and the defender has no luft, one heavy piece can end the game immediately.

## Model Position 2: Finishing with queen and king

<chess-board fen="6k1/5ppp/8/8/8/5Q2/5PPP/6K1 w - - 0 1" highlight="a8,b8,c8,g7,h7,f7" arrows="f3-a8"></chess-board>
*The queen mates most efficiently when the king helps cut off routes.*

Beginners often check endlessly with the queen but never improve the net. The right method is to shrink the enemy king's box first and only then deliver the final check.

## Common Mistakes
- Giving repeated checks that look active but only help the enemy king escape.
- Ignoring stalemate when the opponent has almost no legal moves.
- Forgetting that one escape square can ruin an otherwise attractive mating idea.
- Attacking before enough pieces have joined the action.

## Training Work
- Solve ten mate-in-one positions and explain why every other move fails.
- Practice queen-and-king versus king and rook-and-king versus king until you can finish without panic.
- After each online game, check whether you missed a forcing mate or allowed one.

## Why This Chapter Matters
Mating patterns are the first real tactical vocabulary of chess. Once you know the shapes, your attacks become simpler, faster, and much more accurate.
