Most beginners struggle because the board never becomes familiar enough to think on it calmly. This chapter turns the pieces, squares, and rules into a usable system so the rest of the series can build on something solid.

## What This Chapter Teaches
- Set the board up correctly every time by using light square on the right and queen on her own color.
- Read and speak basic algebraic notation so you can follow books, videos, and your own games.
- Understand how each piece moves, captures, and cooperates with the others.
- Recognize why development, king safety, and central control matter from move one.

## Practical Framework
- Learn the board in layers. First know the coordinates, then the piece moves, then the purpose of each move.
- Say moves out loud when you study. Naming squares is the fastest route to better board vision.
- Do not memorize rules in isolation. Put every rule on a board and play mini positions until it feels natural.
- When you are unsure in a real game, check three things before moving: is my king safe, what is attacked, and what becomes active after my move.

## Model Position 2: Castling is part safety and part development

*Castling changes the king and rook at the same time.*

Many new players see castling as a special rule to memorize. Stronger players see why it matters: the king leaves the center and the rook enters the game without losing time.

## Common Mistakes
- Starting with the board turned the wrong way and then learning the coordinates incorrectly.
- Bringing the queen out early because it looks powerful, while the rest of the army stays asleep.
- Thinking of pawns as disposable when they actually shape the entire battle.
- Ignoring legal move details such as castling rights, check, and the way pawns capture.

## Training Work
- Spend one session naming every square on an empty board until you can do it without pausing.
- Play five miniature games where your only goal is to develop pieces and castle quickly.
- Set up the pieces from memory three times in a row and explain each piece move to someone else or to yourself.

## Why This Chapter Matters
If the board feels familiar, chess becomes a decision game instead of a guessing game. Every advanced idea in this series rests on this first chapter.
