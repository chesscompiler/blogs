The bishop pair is often called an advantage, but it only pays when the position lets both bishops breathe. If the center is closed and your pawns lock your own diagonals, the pair may be ornamental. If the board opens, the pair can dominate both wings at once.

## What This Chapter Teaches
- Understand when the bishop pair is genuinely valuable and when it is mostly cosmetic.
- Use pawn breaks and exchanges to open diagonals for both bishops.
- Know when to keep both bishops and when to trade one for a concrete target.
- Coordinate the bishop pair with rooks and queen to create multi-wing pressure.

## Practical Framework
- Ask whether the position is likely to open and whether both bishops have useful diagonals.
- Preserve the pair when you can increase its range later.
- If the structure stays closed, consider transforming the advantage instead of worshipping it.
- Bishop-pair play is strongest when you create targets on both sides of the board.

*One central change can transform quiet bishops into dominant pieces.*

This is why bishop-pair positions often revolve around timing. The side with the pair wants the structure to open on favorable terms, not just open at random.

## Common Mistakes
- Keeping the bishop pair at all costs when one bishop is strategically useless.
- Closing the center with your own pawns and then claiming a bishop-pair edge.
- Trading the wrong bishop and leaving yourself with only one active diagonal.
- Forgetting that the bishop pair needs targets to matter.

## Training Work
- Review games where the bishop pair became strong only after a later pawn break.
- Practice deciding whether to trade a bishop by writing what changes if the board opens or stays closed.
- Study endgames where bishop range across both wings mattered more than material.

## Why This Chapter Matters
The bishop pair is not a trophy. It is a potential energy source that must be released by structure, diagonals, and targets.
