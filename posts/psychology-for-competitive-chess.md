Psychology in chess is not vague positivity. It is how you respond to pressure, mistakes, ratings, and momentum while still making good decisions. Mental discipline is practical because it changes the quality of the moves you are able to find.

## What This Chapter Teaches
- Recognize emotional triggers such as fear of stronger opponents, tilt after mistakes, and overconfidence in winning positions.
- Use a between-moves routine that returns attention to the board.
- Separate result-based thinking from decision quality.
- Build post-game habits that reduce emotional carryover into the next round.

## Practical Framework
- When emotions rise, shrink your focus to the next position, the next threat, and the next candidate moves.
- Do not interpret one bad move as proof that the whole game is gone.
- Use routines before and after each move so your thinking quality does not depend on mood.
- After the game, record what you felt and how it affected your decisions. That turns psychology into trainable data.

*Even simple positions become dangerous when panic replaces process.*

Psychology shows up most clearly when the move is simple but the player is not calm enough to see it. A routine that resets attention is worth real rating points.

## Common Mistakes
- Measuring every game by result only and never by decision quality.
- Carrying one bad round into the next one mentally.
- Playing the opponent's rating instead of the position.
- Treating nerves as a personal flaw instead of a signal to use better routines.

## Training Work
- Write a simple pre-move checklist and use it in every serious game until it becomes automatic.
- Keep a short emotional log after tournaments to identify recurring triggers.
- Practice resetting after blunders in training games instead of resigning the mental fight.

## Why This Chapter Matters
Chess psychology is useful when it becomes behavioral. The goal is not to feel perfect. The goal is to think clearly anyway.
