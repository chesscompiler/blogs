A repertoire should make your decisions easier, not heavier. Club players often collect openings the way tourists collect souvenirs. A working repertoire is narrower, more practical, and built around positions you actually understand.

## What This Chapter Teaches
- Choose openings based on pawn structures, typical plans, and your own decision style.
- Cover White, Black versus 1.e4, and Black versus 1.d4 or flank openings without overloading yourself.
- Distinguish between knowing lines and knowing positions.
- Create a study file that tracks your main line, your backup line, and your critical sideline answers.

## Practical Framework
- Start with one opening as White and one dependable answer to each major first move as Black.
- Pick openings that repeat structures so your middlegame understanding compounds over time.
- Use engine work to check tactics, but build your notes around plans, pawn breaks, and model games.
- Update your repertoire after losses only when the position itself is a problem, not when you simply played the position badly.

When you know the pawn structure you are aiming for, you do not need to memorize every branch. You know which exchanges help you, which breaks matter, and where your pieces usually belong.

*The move order matters less once you understand the strategic skeleton.*

Players with a stable repertoire recognize these structures instantly. They know whether to play for a space edge, a minority attack, or central simplification before the clock becomes a problem.

## Common Mistakes
- Changing openings every time you lose a game.
- Copying a grandmaster repertoire without the time to maintain it.
- Studying engine lines without collecting model games in the same structure.
- Preparing only your favorite line and ignoring common sidelines.

## Training Work
- Build a one-page repertoire sheet with your main move orders and the middlegame plans that follow.
- For each opening you play, annotate two model games from strong players in plain language.
- After every tournament, add one practical note to your repertoire instead of adding a new opening.

## Why This Chapter Matters
The best repertoire is not the sharpest or the most fashionable. It is the one that gives you positions you can handle repeatedly under tournament conditions.
